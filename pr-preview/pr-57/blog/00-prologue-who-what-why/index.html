Prologue

Who's building this, why, and why I'm posting it

October 6, 2026

In 2024 I left a corporate engineering job to find my place in AI. I went to grad school, worked a library desk and a university accessibility office, then went back to building AI professionally. LibSync is where all of it meets.

The world feels obsessed with the AI rat race, and the reactions run to extremes: hype on one side, panic on the other. I'm not interested in either. I believe we can make AI ethical, interesting and useful, especially for populations technology tends to underserve, like disabled people.

I believe information should be available to everyone and, more importantly, accessible to everyone, and that AI can help close the gap between the two. I'm autistic, and I'm painfully aware of how design choices can lock someone out of a space. For people like me, the social cost of asking a stranger for help can be the barrier. I want an AI that makes asking a library a question easier, with a human always one click away.

That's LibSync, an AI assistant for public libraries. It answers what people ask at a library desk (How much is the late fee? Can I renew this twice? How do I cite that paper?) from sources it can point to, and when it can't check something, it says so. I spent 2025 operating a Sierra ILS behind a public library desk; building LibSync is how I'm exploring the rest of library technology hands-on.

Here's the honest reason I'm talking about any of this: I have a lot left to learn, and I've been trying to learn it in a vacuum. Library technology, agentic coding, accessibility done properly. A vacuum has nobody to tell you you're wrong. So I'm doing it in public: what I build, what breaks, what I get wrong.

I'm the architect. Claude Code is the builder, and it wrote nearly all of the code (the first version, last October, was mine, with another AI agent's help). By day I build production AI agents; this is where I explore the same ideas on my own time, in a field I care about. It's a portfolio project on a $0 budget, built around a full-time job and grad school, with no ops team and no QA department, which is how things fall through the cracks.

The series has three threads: what Claude Code built, what it got wrong (some of it was mine), and what I had to do as the architect. 18 posts, Tuesdays, MIT licensed. Part 1 is the Friday it all broke. Yes, Claude Code helps draft these posts too, and the first draft had a wrong test count, which tells you how the series goes.
